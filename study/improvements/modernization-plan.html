The short answer

Build the modern design once, prove it at 0.4B, then scale

My recommendation is a 411M-parameter model with a 32,768-token context. Train it on about 22 billion tokens on a Colab A100. Go to 3–4B only with rented multi-GPU machines, once that run has proven the recipe.

A model of 3.9B parameters needs 62 GB of training state, against the T4's 16 GB. A Chinchilla-sized run of it would take about 7.6 years of T4 time. The same architecture at 411M fits one Colab A100 and trains in about 181 GPU-hours, which is about $98 in compute units. Its weights and a full 32k KV cache come to 2.2 GB, which fits the MacBook's 4 GB GPU.

Recommended
Model
M · 411M

32 layers × 960, 15 query and 5 key/value heads, SwiGLU, RoPE, RMSNorm and QK-norm. This is SmolLM2-360M's shape with today's vocabulary.

Pretrain at 4,096 tokens. A short second stage at 32,768 with a larger RoPE base follows, the way SmolLM3, Qwen3 and Llama 3 did it.

Data

About 49 tokens per parameter and 17.7 million documents, from FineWeb-Edu, DCLM, The Stack v3 and FineMath. Books, code repositories and long PDFs supply the long stage. All of it is openly downloadable.

Compute
≈ 181 A100-hours

About $98 in Colab compute units. Rented on RunPod: $154 on one H100 over 2.4 days, or $201–$228 for 3–7 hours on 8 GPUs. On the T4 it would take 84 days.

Your 3–4B, 32k target, priced

XL, the Qwen3-4B shape, has 3.89B parameters. A Chinchilla-sized run of 80B tokens plus a 10B-token 32k stage takes about 1,882 H100-hours. On 8 GPUs that is 4.3–9.8 days, and $6,610–$7,510 at RunPod's prices for 8 B200s, H200s or H100s[1, 2]. It does not fit one 80 GB GPU, and RunPod's cheaper Community Cloud rents H100s only one per machine.

It also needs:

  • training code that shards the model across GPUs;
  • about 360 GB of tokenised data;
  • 4-bit quantisation to run on the MacBook at all.

The architecture is the same at every size, so this stays a configuration change once M has proven the code and the recipe.

What holds it back, part by part

  • The position table caps the context. A learned row per position cannot be read beyond its 1,024 rows, and it gives no way to extend the context after training.
  • The block design dates from 2019. LayerNorm, a GELU feed-forward, biases and one key/value head per query head have each been replaced in current models by parts that measure better at the same size. The table under The modern architecture gives the evidence.
  • The model is shallow and wide. At this size, 30 layers beat 12 for the same parameters: MobileLLM's average zero-shot score rose from 43.9 to 44.8[4].
  • Generation has no KV cache. Every new token is a full pass over the whole text, so the MacBook's GPU falls from 12–15 tokens a second on short text to 3.8 at 1,024 tokens (measured 2026-10-12).
  • The schedule fixes the run length up front. The cosine curve to a tenth of the peak is set from the total number of updates, so a run cannot be lengthened once it starts.
  • Training uses fp16 even where bf16 exists. The trainer always uses fp16 with a gradient scaler. That is right on a T4, which has no bf16[5], but wasteful and less stable on an A100 or H100.
Reality check

Why not 3–4B on this hardware

Size has to follow from the compute budget. Three numbers decide whether a size is trainable at all: memory, time and data.

Memory

XL needs 62 GB before a single activation is stored. A T4 has 16 GB[5]. Even 8-bit optimiser states, at about 2 bytes per parameter for Adam's two moments, leave it far over. Sharding across GPUs divides the state by the number of GPUs[6].

Time

The T4 delivered about 11.2 TFLOPS on today's model, measured from the 11,400 tokens a second of the 256-token run. At that rate XL needs 666 hours per billion tokens. Even your 5B-token dataset would take 139 days.

Data per parameter

5B tokens is 1.3 tokens per parameter for XL. The compute-optimal ratio is about 20[7], and current small models go far beyond it. At a fixed budget, a smaller model trained on enough data beats a large undertrained one[7, 8].

On the MacBook, XL's fp16 weights alone are 7.8 GB, about twice the GPU's memory. Qwen3-4B, the same shape, is 2.5 GB at 4-bit[9], and its fp16 KV cache at 32k is another 4.8 GB. A user report says llama.cpp's Metal backend gives garbage output on this GPU, while its Vulkan backend works[10].

Design

The modern architecture

Current small open models (Qwen3, SmolLM2 and SmolLM3, OLMo 2, Llama 3.2, Gemma 3) have converged on one decoder layout. The proposal adopts it with no exotic parts, so every component has published evidence at small scale. It also keeps the layer layout of Qwen3, which may later allow export to common inference tools.

How much the design matters at a fixed size shows in the NanoGPT speedrun. It reaches the loss llm.c's GPT-2 small reached on 10B tokens with under 330M tokens, using rotary embeddings, QK-norm and the Muon optimiser among other changes[11].

Considered and left out at this size

  • Mixture of experts. OLMoE matched a dense model with about 3× fewer tokens but was only about 2× faster in wall-clock time. It must also hold all 7B parameters in memory[26], which the T4 and the MacBook cannot.
  • Multi-token prediction. It did worse than plain next-token training at small sizes and helped only from about 13B[27].
  • Sliding-window attention. Gemma 3's 5 local layers per global layer cut KV memory a lot[28], but at sizes S and M the full 32k cache is only 0.75 and 1.34 GB. It is worth considering for XL.
  • No-position layers. SmolLM3 drops RoPE in every 4th layer and reports better long context[29]. It is a cheap option for the 32k stage, but the published comparison gives no numbers.
  • The Muon optimiser. It needs about 52% of AdamW's compute in Moonshot's tests[30]. An independent benchmark found its lead shrinks from 1.4× at 0.1B to 1.1× at 1.2B[31], and no source tests it in fp16. It is a candidate for a short test at size S, not the default.
Context window

From 1,024 to 32,768 tokens

A long context is cheap to add late and expensive to train from the start. Current models pretrain at 4–8k and then extend in a short stage with a larger RoPE base. SmolLM3 went from 4k to 32k with 50B tokens at base 1.5M, then to 64k[29]. Qwen3 raised its base from 10,000 to 1,000,000 for a 32,768-token stage[15]. Llama 3 reached 128k in six stages with about 800B tokens[23].

The data has to contain long documents

Measured on your 1-billion-token FineWeb mix: the median document is 476 tokens, and only 1.5% of the tokens sit in documents of 32k or more. A 32k window built from this data is mostly unrelated pages packed together, which teaches nothing about long-range reading.

The long stage therefore draws on books, whole code repositories and long PDFs. ProLong found code repositories and books the best long sources, and a mix of 60% long with 40% short data the best[24]. Olmo 3 kept short-context scores within 0.8 points using 34% long data[32]. In Fu et al.'s experiments, 0.5–5B tokens of such training were enough for retrieval anywhere in a 128k context[33]. The plan for M uses 2B tokens at 32k, 40% of them long.

What the code needs for 32k

  • Loss in chunks. The full logits of one 32,768-token sequence over 100,352 tokens are 13.2 GB in fp32. The output layer and loss have to run a slice of the sequence at a time.
  • Activation checkpointing. Keeping only each layer's input cuts activation memory to 2 × sequence × width × layers bytes[34]. For M at 32k that is 2.0 GB.
  • Document masking. PyTorch's FlexAttention or a variable-length kernel do this efficiently on Ampere and newer GPUs. On a T4, an explicit mask at 4k is possible, but I haven't measured its cost.
  • Beyond 32k. YaRN can stretch a trained context further at inference; SmolLM3 reaches 128k that way[35, 29]. I haven't verified this at our sizes.

Renting GPUs on RunPod

Prices from RunPod's pricing page, checked against its public API, on 2026-10-16[1, 2]. The API also gives how many GPUs one machine can have. Community Cloud rents the H100 SXM only one per machine. Eight H100s are therefore Secure Cloud only, at $31.92 an hour. Eight H200s on Community Cloud cost $28.72 an hour, and NVIDIA lists the same bf16 peak for the H200 as for the H100[44].

The B200 has 2.25 PFLOPS of dense bf16, 2.28× the H100[45]. RunPod rents it on Secure Cloud at $7.99, up to 8 per machine. At equal utilisation that costs about the same per unit of training as Community H200s ($7.99 against 2.28 × $3.59 = $8.17), and it finishes in less than half the time. Its 180 GB could also hold XL's 62 GB of training state on one GPU, though one B200 would take about 34 days. I haven't checked that XL's activations fit as well. The catch is utilisation: a model as narrow as M is less likely to reach 40% of a B200's peak than of an H100's, and at 25% the B200 run would cost 1.6 times as much. Its Community price of $5.98 is listed, but the API reported no Community machine with a B200.

The cheapest change: stop training on the T4

Per compute unit, an A100 40 GB delivers about 2.4× the training of a T4 (23 against 9.4 TFLOPS-hours per unit). It is also about 11× faster, and it has bf16 and FlashAttention, which the T4 lacks[47, 48]. The ratio holds even at half the assumed utilisation. Colab lists A100, H100, L4 and G4 alongside the T4, subject to availability[42].

Why M is my pick

It is the largest size that still trains on one GPU in about a week, fits Colab's A100 and needs no multi-GPU code. Its weights and 32k cache also fit the MacBook's GPU. Its shape is proven: SmolLM2-360M reached HellaSwag 54.5 with 4T tokens[49].

At 22B tokens it will be far below that, and I don't predict its score. What it will show is whether the modern recipe beats today's model at equal compute. That answer should come before anyone spends $6,610 on XL.

Data

How much data, and where to get it

The compute-optimal amount is about 20 tokens per parameter[7]. Small models now train far past it: SmolLM2-360M on 4T tokens, SmolLM3-3B on 11.2T and Qwen3 on 36T[49, 29, 15]. Repeating data for up to about 4 epochs costs almost nothing[52]. So your 5B tokens can serve S for several passes, but M and larger need new data.

Bytes per token, higher is better. Measured 2026-10-16 on the 1,352 validation documents (4.9 MB) and this repository's 40 Python files (111 kB).

cl100k compresses about as well as any of these vocabularies, up to 200k, and ties for best on code. It needs no new dependency and keeps the 5B tokens already prepared. Its cost is the table: 35% of S and 23% of M. A 49k vocabulary would shrink that and halve storage, since token ids would fit in 2 bytes. It would also need 4–7% more tokens on English and 25% more on code. That trade is listed under Decisions for you.

Inference

Running it on the MacBook

The MacBook has an Intel CPU, a Radeon Pro 5300M with 4 GB, 16 GB of RAM and PyTorch 2.2.2, the last release for Intel Macs.

SizeWeights in fp16KV cache at 32kTogetherOn the 4 GB Radeon Pro 5300M
S · 164M0.33 GB0.75 GB1.08 GBfits in fp16 with PyTorch; long prompts go in chunks
M · 411M0.82 GB1.34 GB2.16 GBfits in fp16 with PyTorch; long prompts go in chunks
L · 1.61B3.23 GB3.76 GB6.99 GBdoes not fit; needs 4-bit weights and a quantised KV cache, or the CPU
XL · 3.89B7.78 GB4.83 GB12.61 GBdoes not fit; needs 4-bit weights and a quantised KV cache, or the CPU
  • KV cache. Each new token then costs one position instead of a pass over the whole text, which is what makes 32k usable at all.
  • Gaps in PyTorch 2.2.2, measured 2026-10-16. The model code has to work around them:
    • no built-in RMSNorm, so the model defines its own;
    • no grouped-query option in its attention function, so keys and values are repeated across their query heads;
    • no FlexAttention;
    • no bf16 on the GPU, so checkpoints trained in bf16 are converted to fp16 for the MacBook.
  • Long prompts in chunks. PyTorch 2.2.2's attention on this GPU stores the whole score matrix (measured 2026-10-16): one head took 128 MiB at 4k tokens, 512 MiB at 8k and 2,048 MiB at 16k. At 32k one head would need about 8 GiB, so a long prompt has to go into the KV cache a few hundred tokens at a time. I haven't run a full 32k prompt on the MacBook.
  • Context length from the checkpoint. generate.py should read the context length and shape from the checkpoint, not from the global config. Today it cannot load the 256-token files.
  • L and XL. These need 4-bit weights. A user report says llama.cpp's Metal backend produces garbage on this GPU and its Vulkan backend works[10]. That route also needs an export to a format llama.cpp reads, which I haven't tried.
Plan

Roadmap

Each step is one reviewable pull request, in this order, and each ends with something measured.

  1. Measure today's model

    Add a HellaSwag, ARC and PIQA evaluation next to validation loss, and run it on the step-16,000 checkpoint and on the first 1,024-token checkpoints. Verifies: a baseline to beat. Checking the harness against a published score needs a way to load a published model, which may need a new dependency.

  2. The modern model

    RMSNorm, RoPE, SwiGLU, grouped-query attention, QK-norm, no biases and a tied table, all set by a size config (S, M, L, XL). Verifies: parameter counts equal this report's formula, and the published MobileLLM counts with their vocabulary. Later tokens never change earlier outputs. RoPE scores depend only on relative position. 5 KV heads repeated equal 15 full heads with copied weights. A forward pass runs on the MacBook's GPU in fp16.

  3. Generation with a KV cache

    Cache keys and values, read the shape and context from the checkpoint, and use fp16 on the MacBook. Verifies: cached and uncached greedy generation give identical tokens, and speed at 1k, 8k and 32k is measured.

  4. Trainer upgrades

    WSD schedule, bf16 or fp16 by GPU, z-loss, chunked loss, no decay on the table, activation checkpointing, document masking where supported, data-parallel training. Verifies: a resumed run equals an uninterrupted one, as for today's trainer, and the schedule can be extended from the pre-decay checkpoint.

  5. Data pipeline for several sources

    A staged mixer for web, code and maths, tokenised into shards that stream from storage, plus a long-document builder for the 32k stage. Verifies: the mix proportions, document counts and separators of each shard against its manifest.

  6. Pilot at size S

    Train S on the existing 5B tokens, with a short learning-rate sweep, and set it against today's model at equal tokens. Verifies: that the modern recipe wins at equal compute, before money goes into M.

  7. Main run at size M

    20B tokens at 4k, then 2B at 32k, on a Colab A100, one rented H100 or 8 rented H200s. Verifies: benchmarks against the pilot and today's model, and long-context retrieval at 4k to 32k.

  8. Instruction tuning

    SmolTalk, then optionally a preference pass. Verifies: the model answers prompts instead of continuing them.

  9. Decide on L or XL

    With M's measured results and a rental budget, add sharded multi-GPU training and run the larger size. Verifies: the cost per token against this report's estimate.

Your call

Decisions for you

  1. Compute budget. The options are T4 only, paid Colab compute units on A100s, or rented GPUs. This choice sets the size: S on a T4 takes about 10 days, M costs about $98 on Colab's A100 or $154 on one rented H100, and XL costs $6,610–$7,510 on 8 rented GPUs.
  2. Target size. M is my recommendation. XL is your stated preference.
  3. Tokenizer. Keeping cl100k is my recommendation. A ≤65k vocabulary would mean smaller small models and half the storage, but worse code compression, re-tokenising the 5B tokens and a new dependency.
  4. Where the tokenised data lives. M needs 88 GB. The options are a larger Drive plan, a private Hugging Face dataset, or tokenising on the training machine each time.
  5. Context target. 32k is my recommendation. A second stage to 64k would follow SmolLM3.
  6. Licences. If the model will be shared, decide whether data and instruction sets with attribution, share-alike or non-commercial terms are acceptable.
  7. New dependencies. I will ask step by step, for example for an evaluation harness or for checking against published models.
Limits of this report

What is not verified

  • Colab rates. Compute units per hour come from one third-party measurement in March 2026. The H100 rate is unknown, and A100 access on paid plans is subject to availability[77, 42].
  • Utilisation. The 40% assumed for the A100 and H100 is not measured for these shapes; published comparable runs range from 29% to 60%. The T4 rate was measured only on today's model at 256-token windows.
  • torch.compile and masking on the T4. Whether torch.compile helps on a T4 is untested, since Triton officially supports compute capability 8.0 and up[78]. The cost of document masking on a T4 is also unmeasured.
  • Long context at small sizes. Published long-context recipes come from 3B–8B models; none is tested at 164M–411M.
  • Muon in fp16. No source tests it.
  • Scores. The benchmark results of S, M, L and XL at these token counts are not predicted here; step 1 and the pilot will measure them.
  • Prices and stock. Seen 2026-10-16; they change. RunPod's stock was low for almost every GPU, and no 8-GPU H100 or H200 machine was on offer when checked.
  • H200 and B200 speed. The H200 is costed at the H100's speed because their listed bf16 peaks are equal; its larger memory bandwidth may make it faster. The B200 is costed at the same 40% of its peak as the others, which a model as small as M may not reach. Neither is measured.
  • Document counts. DCLM's and The Stack v3's cards don't name their tokenizer, so those two counts may be off by a few percent. Sampling more rows stopped when Hugging Face's row service began refusing requests.
  • Exporting checkpoints. Running our checkpoints in llama.cpp or Hugging Face libraries has not been tried.
